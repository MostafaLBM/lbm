From the picture to the equation

The Joule–Thomson coefficient is the slope of a constant-enthalpy line at one state:

μJT=(∂T∂p)h=vcp(Tα−1)

α = (1/v)(∂v/∂T)p is the thermal expansion coefficient; v is specific volume. Temperature in this equation is absolute.

μ > 0 → coolingdp < 0, so dT < 0.

μ < 0 → heatingdp < 0, so dT > 0.

μ = 0 → horizontal tangentThe tangent is horizontal.

Why an inlet slope is not the whole answer

For a small pressure drop, ΔT ≈ μJT,1 Δp. Over a large drop, μ changes along the isenthalp, so the outlet is found by solving h(T₂,p₂) = h(T₁,p₁). A path can even cross an inversion point. The app solves for the outlet; it does not extrapolate the inlet tangent.

The displayed inlet μ is a single-phase derivative. A two-phase outlet can still be calculated from pressure and enthalpy, but the single-phase derivative formula must not be applied to that mixture.

Where does the coefficient come from?
dh=cpdT+[v−T(∂v∂T)p]dp

Set dh = 0, isolate dT/dp, and use (∂v/∂T)p = αv. For an ideal gas, v = RT/p and α = 1/T, so μJT = 0. This remains true when the ideal-gas specific heat varies with temperature.

Where does flashing get its energy?

Some liquid vaporizes as the pressure falls. The energy for this phase change comes from redistribution of the stream’s own enthalpy; no external heater is needed. The outlet temperature and vapor mass fraction are determined together by its pressure and enthalpy.

Model, limits & further reading

Steady adiabatic throttling of a pure fluid, with no shaft work and negligible changes in bulk kinetic and potential energy. Real-fluid states use CoolProp 7.2.0, shared with the refrigeration applet. Hydrogen uses the normal-hydrogen equation of state; ortho–para conversion is not modeled. Solid phases are not modeled. Control ranges are teaching ranges, not the full validity envelope of each equation of state; unavailable states are reported explicitly.

The inversion overlay is found numerically from single-phase zeros of μJT over the selected fluid’s control range. It may show only part of the inversion locus. A gap is not evidence that an inversion point cannot exist elsewhere.
